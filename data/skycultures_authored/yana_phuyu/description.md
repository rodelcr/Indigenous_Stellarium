# Yana Phuyu

## Introduction

**This is a literature-derived draft. It is not community-authored, it has not
been reviewed by Quechua or other Andean community members, and community
sources supersede it.**

Every name, gloss and description below is taken from a published source that
was retrieved and read while compiling this file; each statement carries an
inline reference. Nothing here was reconstructed from memory, and nothing was
translated or re-spelled by the compiler. Where sources disagree — and on
orthography they routinely do — the variants are recorded side by side and
attributed, rather than harmonised into a single "correct" form.

*Yana phuyu* ("dark cloud") is the Quechua term for a class of constellation
that is defined not by joining stars but by the dark dust lanes silhouetted
against the bright band of the Milky Way [#1] [#3]. Because these figures are
outlined by obscuring dust rather than by stars, **this file deliberately
contains no `lines` and no constellation artwork**: no source consulted gives
star-by-star membership or a polygon for any of these figures, so drawing one
would mean inventing it. The two star names that *are* attested in the
literature are recorded, and nothing else. See "Description" below for the
reasoning.

A community-authored visual representation is being sought. If one is
contributed, it would be carried in this format's `image` + three-HIP `anchors`
mechanism, and it — not this literature summary — would be the culture's
depiction. The two layers are complementary: this file carries names,
orthographic variants, cited descriptions and references; an artwork would
carry the picture.

## Description

### What a *yana phuyu* is

Gary Urton's ethnoastronomical fieldwork in Misminay, a community near Cusco,
Peru, records that Quechua speakers there recognise two major types of
constellation: star-to-star groupings, which represent "inanimate architectural
and geometric forms", and *yana phuyu* ("dark cloud") constellations, "all of
which represent *animate* forms" [#1, p. 111]. The dark cloud constellations
lie in the southern portion of the Milky Way, "where one sees the densest
clustering of stars and the greatest surface brightness, and where therefore,
the fixed clouds of interstellar dust which cut through the center of the Milky
Way (the dark cloud constellations) appear in sharper contrast" [#1, p. 111].

The Milky Way itself is *Mayu*, "the River" [#1, p. 111]; the Smithsonian's
Great Inka Road exhibition likewise records that "throughout the Andes, the
Milky Way was believed to be a river, Mayu, the source of all water on earth"
[#3].

Urton reports that constellations in Misminay are further divided into three
colour categories — *yana* ("dark"), *muru* ("spotted" or "multi-colored") and
*rojo* ("red") — and that his 1981 article treats the "dark" and "spotted"
animal forms only [#1, p. 111].

### Where they lie

The dark cloud figures form a single line along the Milky Way. Urton: "From the
head of the Serpent in the west to the tail of the Tinamou in the east, the
dark cloud constellations stretch in a line through about 150° of celestial
space, straight along the central course of the Milky Way" [#1, p. 111]. Gullberg
et al. give the same figure: "The pantheon of Inca dark constellations stretches
nearly 150° across the Milky Way" [#2, p. 394].

Urton lists them "in the order in which they rise above the south-eastern
horizon" [#1, p. 111]:

    mach'acuay      —serpent
    hanp'atu        —toad
    yutu            —tinamou
    llama           —llama
    uñallamacha     —baby llama (/or llama's umbilicus/or serpent)
    atoq            —fox
    yuthu           —tinamou

The whole line is most visible "when the center of the line (i.e., the area
around the Southern Cross and the *Yutu*) stands along the north/south meridian
at midnight; this occurs around the 23rd of March" [#1, p. 111].

### Why this file has no outlines

The extents of these figures are given in the sources as prose relative to
Western constellations and to a small number of named stars — "from a point near
the Southern Cross to Adhara", "at the foot of the Southern Cross", "on the
ecliptic between Scorpius and Sagittarius". None of the sources consulted
publishes star-level membership, a boundary polygon, or coordinates for any dark
cloud figure, and by their nature these figures are dust silhouettes rather than
stellar patterns. Synthesising `lines` from the prose would produce geometry
that no source supports, so the constellation entries in `index.json` carry a
name and nothing else.

Two star names *are* attested, and only two. Urton records that "α and β
Centaurii are referred to as *llamacñawin* ('the eyes of the llama')" [#1,
p. 122]; Gullberg et al. repeat this, citing Urton [#2, p. 395]; the Smithsonian
gives "Llamacñawin ... Eye of the llama" and "the bright stars Alpha and Beta
Centauri serve as the llama's eyes" [#3]; Pinasco gives the same identification
with the spelling *Llamaqñawin* [#4, pp. 253–254, 260]. Those two stars are
therefore recorded in `common_names`, resolved to HIP 71683 (α Centauri A) and
HIP 68702 (β Centauri) via SIMBAD [#6]. Note that the sources name the **pair**;
the name is attached to each of the two stars here because that is the only way
this format can express it.

## Constellations

Entries follow Urton's rising order [#1, p. 111], with *Micheq* appended
because it is attested by the Smithsonian but not by Urton (see below).

##### Mach'acuay

Serpent. Urton: "The principal identification of the dark cloud Serpent
(*Mach'acuay*) is a large zig-zag-like streak of interstellar dust which
stretches from a point near the Southern Cross to Adhara (in the Western
constellation of Canis Majoris). The head of the Serpent precedes the tail in
rising" [#1, p. 118]. He gives the heliacal rise of the head as the first week
of August and its heliacal set as the first week of February, with *Mach'acuay*
on the north/south meridian at midnight on 1 February [#1, p. 118]. It is seen
in the sky at night during the rainy season and is below ground at night during
the dry season (May–July) [#1, p. 111]. Gullberg et al.: "The serpent's dark
figure is long, like a snake, and travels head before the tail across the sky"
[#2, p. 394]. The Smithsonian: "*Mach'acuay* was said to oversee all snakes on
Earth. This constellation emerges head-first in August and begins to set in
February, which matches the activity of real snakes in the Andes" [#3].

*The Adhara endpoint rests on Urton alone.* (Adhara = ε Canis Majoris = HIP
33579 [#6]; the identification of the star is astronomical, not cultural — Urton
uses it as a Western reference point and does not report a Quechua name for it,
so no star name is recorded for it here.)

##### Hanp'atu

Toad. Urton: "a small patch of inter-stellar dust in the Milky Way which moves
between the tail of *Mach'acuay* and the Southern Cross"; in the first days of
October it "rises about one and one-half hours before the sun" [#1, p. 119].
Gullberg et al.: "a much smaller dark section of the Milky Way to the left of
the snake" [#2, p. 395]. The Smithsonian: "The toad was a very important animal
for the Inka. They believed that the more toads croaked, the more likely it was
that rain would soon begin. The appearance of the toad in the sky meant that it
was time to plant" [#3].

##### Yutu

Tinamou. Urton: "Near the center of the line of Dark Cloud constellations, at
the point where the Milky Way flows nearest to the south celestial pole, sits
the Tinamou (*Yutu*). The *Yutu* of Quechua astronomy is equivalent to the
Western constellation of the Coalsack" [#1, p. 119], and is "located at the foot
of the group of stars which, in Western astronomy, is known as the Southern
Cross (Crux)", so that its periodicities are "virtually identical" to those of
Crux [#1, p. 120]. Gullberg et al.: "*Yutu*, the Tinamou (the Coalsack) follows
*Hanpatu* in the Milky Way and likewise is much smaller than *Machacuay*"
[#2, p. 395]. The Smithsonian: "this kite-shaped constellation seems to pursue
the constellation of the toad ... It appears in the sky in October and stays
until July, its disappearance marking the end of the potato harvest" [#3].

*The Coalsack identification is the one dark-cloud identification given by
multiple independent sources.*

##### Yacana

Llama. Urton calls it "perhaps the most conspicuous Dark Cloud constellation,
since it virtually fills the sky overhead during the rainy season" [#1, p. 122].
Its eyes are α and β Centauri, *llamacñawin*, "the first part of the huge body
of the llama to appear over the horizon"; after their heliacal rising in late
November the body rises higher each morning until, in late April, "the Dark
Cloud constellation of the Llama stands along the north/south meridian at
midnight" [#1, p. 122]. Gullberg et al.: "*Yacana* is situated between Centaurus
and Scorpius" and "is much larger than *Hanpatu* or *Yutu* and dominates the
Incas' dark section of the Milky Way" [#2, p. 395]. The name *Yacana* comes from
the early-seventeenth-century Huarochirí manuscript of Francisco de Avila, which
Urton quotes: "This *Yacana* [in the sky] ... is like the shadow of the llama.
They say that this *Yacana* comes down to earth at midnight when it cannot be
noticed or seen and drinks all the water from the sea. They say that if she did
not drink this water, the entire world would be drowned" [Avila 1608, ch. 29,
quoted and translated in #1, p. 113]. Pinasco quotes the same chapter from
Taylor's edition: "The black patch we call *Yacana*, the *cámac* or celestial
prototype which gives vital force to the llamas ... walks down the middle of a
river (the Milky Way). ... It has two eyes and a very long neck" [Taylor 2011,
pp. 121–122, quoted and translated in #4, p. 260].

*The Huarochirí manuscript itself was not retrieved; it is cited here only as
quoted by #1 and #4.*

##### Uñallamacha

Baby llama. Urton's list glosses it "baby llama (/or llama's umbilicus/or
serpent)" [#1, p. 111] and his figure 1 places it between the Llama and
*Mach'acuay* [#1, p. 112, fig. 1]. He also quotes Garcilaso de la Vega, who
reported that Andean observers "saw the figure of an ewe [llama] with the body
complete suckling a lamb [*uñallamacha*], in some dark patches spread over what
the astrologers call the Milky Way" [Garcilaso 1609, Bk. II, ch. XXIII, quoted
in #1, p. 110]. Gullberg et al.: "Below *Yacana* is a smaller dark constellation
called *Uñallamacha*. It is said to be a baby llama suckling its mother"
[#2, p. 395]. The Smithsonian: "The constellation consists of two llamas, mother
and baby, with the baby below the mother, nursing" [#3].

##### Atoq

Fox. Urton: "The Dark Cloud constellation of the *atoq* (Fox) is a rather
amorphous dark spot which stretches at a right angle from the tail of Scorpio
crossing the ecliptic between the Western constellations of Scorpio and
Sagittarius", and the sun "'enters' the constellation of the fox at the time of
the December solstice" [#1, p. 124]. He notes the fox's position "in relation to
the baby llama and the hind legs of the mother llama" as a "motif of pursuit and
trampling" fixed in the interstellar dust [#1, p. 123]. Gullberg et al.: "*Atoq*
lies on the ecliptic between the constellations of Scorpius and Sagittarius. The
Sun enters it during the December solstice" [#2, p. 395]. The Smithsonian: "The
fox is found at the feet of the llama as if it were chasing her. Look closely:
the celestial llama seems to be kicking the fox. The sun passes through this
constellation in December, the time baby foxes are born" [#3].

##### Yuthu

A second tinamou, at the eastern end of the line. Urton lists it as the last of
the seven, spelled *yuthu*, and places it beyond *atoq* in figure 1 [#1, p. 111
and p. 112, fig. 1], but the article gives no separate description of its
extent. Gullberg et al.: "Urton then lists a second constellation called *Yutu*.
This additional tinamou follows *Atoq* and completes the celestial procession"
[#2, p. 395], and their figure 4 caption numbers the seven as "(1) *Machacuay*,
(2) *Hanp'atu*, (3) *Yutu*, (4) *Yacana*, (5) *Uñallamacha*, (6) *Atoq*, and
(7) *Yutu*" [#2, p. 395].

*Whether the spelling difference between* yutu *and* yuthu *in Urton's list is
meaningful or a typographic slip is not something the compiler can determine.
Both spellings are preserved.*

##### Micheq

Shepherd. **Not in Urton's list.** The Smithsonian's Great Inka Road exhibition
names it among the dark cloud figures — "Micheq (Shepherd)" — and describes it:
"Many say the shepherd is a woman, since in Andean communities women own the
llama flocks while the men take care of them. This shepherd extends her arms
towards the llamas, and her legs are close to the paws of the fox" [#3]. The
NASE/IAU teaching slides also list "The Shepherd" as the seventh figure, in
place of Urton's second tinamou [#5]. Gullberg et al.'s published list does not
include a shepherd [#2, p. 395].

*This entry rests on #3, with weak support from #5. It is included because it is
attested in a museum exhibition developed with Quechua-language content, and
flagged because the strongest academic source for the rest of the list does not
have it.*
